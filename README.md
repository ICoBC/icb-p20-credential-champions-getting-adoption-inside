# Credential Champions: Getting Adoption Inside Your Organisation

*Stakeholders, champions and the move from pilot to routine.*

## About this course

Many credential programmes launch well and then stall. The badge design is sound and the platform works, yet managers do not release time, teaching staff do not link the badge to their courses, and the pilot ends without a plan for what comes next. This course teaches you to plan and run adoption so that a credential programme becomes part of how your organisation works.

**Who this course is for**

- Learning and development (L&D) and corporate academy leads who want employees and managers to use internal credentials.
- Programme and micro-credential leads in universities, colleges and training providers.
- Staff of professional bodies and sector associations who need member organisations to recognise and use their credentials.

**Prerequisite:** ICB-F01 Digital Badges and Credentials Essentials.

**Framework alignment:** ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF), skill ADPT "Adoption and change leadership" (VRITE pillar: Impact), level L3 "Enable", stage A "Ability". At this level you plan and run adoption for a programme, build a champion network and manage stakeholder concerns. At the Ability stage you practise this on realistic tasks in a controlled setting, here a set of cases and one project on a real programme.

**Learning outcomes.** After this course you can:

1. Map stakeholders and their interests in a credential programme.
2. Build a champion network and a communication plan.
3. Handle common objections from managers, teaching staff or employee representatives.
4. Plan the move from pilot to routine operation.

**Workload.** 6 hours of notional learning in a blended cohort. This file covers the self-paced part. Live or workshop elements appear as facilitator notes.

**How it is assessed.** You write an adoption plan for a real programme. A person assesses it with the rubric in "Assessment and badge". A short knowledge check in the same section covers all four outcomes. Quizzes in the modules are for practice and are not graded.

**Badge.** "Credential Champions: Getting Adoption Inside Your Organisation (Ability, L3)", alignment code ICSF-ADPT-L3-A. The badge evidence is your adoption plan. Completing the course shows that you have practised and evidenced these adoption skills. It does not by itself prove competence in any job.

## Stakeholders

In this module you will learn to identify who is affected by a credential programme, what each group wants and fears, and how much attention each group needs. You finish by producing a stakeholder map for a programme of your own.

### Who counts as a stakeholder

A stakeholder is any person or group that can influence your credential programme or is affected by it. In an organisation, the group is larger than most programme leads expect. A badge programme for customer service skills, for example, touches the earners, their line managers, the trainers, the HR team, the IT team that runs the platform, the employee representatives and the senior sponsor who pays for it.

Credential programmes have a feature that makes stakeholder work harder than in many projects: the value of a badge depends on others. A badge only means something if people outside the programme team trust it and use it. Managers must recognise it in appraisals or hiring. Teaching staff must accept it as part of a course. Employers or partner bodies must read it as a signal. So you are building recognition, not only delivering a product.

Start by listing groups, not individuals. Use these prompts:

- **Earners:** who will take the credential, and who may be excluded or reluctant?
- **Line managers:** who release time and decide whether the badge counts?
- **Providers of learning:** trainers, lecturers, programme teams and assessors.
- **Decision makers:** sponsors, heads of department, deans, boards.
- **Enablers:** IT, data protection, legal, quality assurance, communications.
- **Representatives:** works councils, trade unions, staff associations, student bodies.
- **External parties:** employers who read the badge, partner institutions, professional bodies, funders.

In Germany and in many other countries, employee representatives have formal rights over matters such as staff data and monitoring. <!-- VERIFY: add the relevant national legal reference for the audience's country before publishing; do not cite articles from memory. --> Treat them as stakeholders from the first week, not as a hurdle at the end.

### Interests, influence and attitude

Once you have a list, describe each group in three short statements: what they want from the programme, what they fear, and how much they can help or block it. Keep the statements concrete. "Wants evidence that staff time on the programme improves service" is more useful than "interested in quality".

Then place the groups on a simple grid with two axes: influence over the programme's success and current level of support. Four zones result.

The map is a working tool for you and your sponsor. Do not publish it, since it records your judgements about people's attitudes. Review it every month in the early phase, because positions change.

### Worked examples for stakeholder mapping

**Worked example: Internal leadership badge in a corporate academy**

The academy of a mid-sized manufacturing company, which we call Norden Works, plans an internal badge for team leaders. The programme lead, Ana, lists the groups and writes one line each (all details are illustrative).

| Group | Wants | Fears | Influence | Support |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant managers | Team leaders who handle shifts well | Lost shift cover during training | High | Low |
| Head of HR | Clear career steps, fair promotion criteria | Badge used unfairly in pay decisions | High | Medium |
| Works council | Fair treatment, no hidden monitoring | Badge data used to rank staff | High | Medium |
| Trainers | Recognition for their work | Extra assessment workload | Medium | High |
| Team leaders (earners) | Visible career progress | Another test to fail | Medium | Medium |
| IT | Few new systems to support | Integration effort | Low | Low |

Ana sees that plant managers and the works council sit in "engage closely". She decides to meet each plant manager individually in week 1 and to invite the works council to co-write the data use statement before launch. She leaves IT in "inform and listen" and agrees one short technical meeting.

**Worked example: Micro-credential in a university continuing education unit**

A university continuing education unit, which we call Hartwell, offers a micro-credential in data literacy. The programme lead, Dr Okafor, finds that the academic departments, not the unit, decide whether the micro-credential can count towards a degree. The faculty quality board, the departmental programme directors and the student services team are in the high-influence zone, and none of them has been consulted. She also lists external employers (low influence on the programme, high influence on its value) and moves them into "keep informed and use as voices", planning to ask two of them for short statements about how they read the badge.

**Practice activity: Map your stakeholders**

Choose a real credential programme (your own or one you know well). If you have none, use the Norden Works or Hartwell case and invent plausible details.

1. List at least eight stakeholder groups using the prompts above.
2. For each group write what they want, what they fear and their influence and support (high, medium or low).
3. Place the groups on a grid like the one above. Name the two groups that most need your attention in the next month and explain why.
4. Mark one group you had not considered before this module.

Keep your map. It becomes part A of your adoption plan.

## Champions

A champion is a person who supports the programme in their own area, speaks for it with credibility and helps others take part. This module shows how to find champions, give them a clear role and keep them going, so that adoption does not rest on one programme lead.

### What a champion does

A programme lead cannot be everywhere. Champions extend your reach into departments, teams and courses where you have no standing. They answer questions, show colleagues what the credential looks like in practice, pass on concerns and model use of the badge in their own work. Colleagues often trust a peer more than a message from a central team.

A champion is not a salesperson, and the role should not be a hidden extra job. Define it in writing with five parts:

1. **Purpose:** what the champion helps to achieve in their area.
2. **Activities:** for example run a short team briefing, share a badge on a professional profile, gather questions, join a monthly call.
3. **Time:** how many hours per month, agreed with their line manager.
4. **Support:** what you give them, such as talking points, a contact person and early information.
5. **Recognition:** how the organisation acknowledges the work, for example mention in appraisal, a development opportunity or a visible thank you.

Without time and recognition, champions drift away within a few months. Agree both before you ask.

### Finding and choosing champions

Look for people who are respected, curious and close to the work. Seniority matters less than credibility. A good champion network usually includes a mix:

- **Sponsors:** senior people who give permission and remove obstacles.
- **Peer champions:** respected practitioners in teams or departments.
- **Expert voices:** trainers, lecturers or assessors who can speak about quality.
- **Critical friends:** sceptics who will tell you honestly what does not work.
- **Representatives:** where relevant, a person from the employee or student body.

Include at least one critical friend. A network made only of enthusiasts will not warn you about problems.

Use your stakeholder map. For each high-influence group, ask who inside it is trusted and could help. Approach people personally, explain why you chose them and say what you ask in time and effort. Allow them to decline.

### Keeping a champion network alive

A network needs a rhythm. A monthly 30-minute call, a shared channel and a short briefing before every milestone are usually enough. Use the call for three things: share news before it goes public, collect questions and concerns, and let champions swap what has worked.

Watch for three common problems:

- **Overload:** one enthusiastic person ends up doing everything. Spread tasks and review time every quarter.
- **Silence:** champions stop attending. Ask them directly what would make the role worth their time.
- **Echo chamber:** only good news travels. Ask each champion to bring one concern to every call.

Plan renewal too. In a pilot, champions may serve for six months, then you ask who will continue and recruit new ones for the next wave.

### Worked examples for champion networks

**Worked example: Champions for a corporate academy badge**

Ana at Norden Works needs the plant managers to release team leaders for the leadership badge. She recruits:

- One sponsor: the operations director, who agrees to open the kick-off and to mention the badge at the quarterly managers' meeting.
- Three peer champions: one team leader from each of three plants who completed the pilot, each given two hours a month agreed with their manager.
- One expert voice: the lead trainer.
- One critical friend: a plant manager who has said the programme takes people off the floor.
- One representative: a works council member who asked to be kept informed.

She writes a one-page champion role description and gets each line manager to confirm the time in writing. Recognition: the operations director sends a thank-you note, and the HR team notes the role in development reviews.

**Worked example: Champions in a university or training provider**

At Hartwell, Dr Okafor needs departmental programme directors to accept the data literacy micro-credential as a module option. She recruits one lecturer from each of three departments who taught on the pilot, one student representative and the head of quality assurance as sponsor. She gives the lecturers a short briefing pack describing learning outcomes, assessment and how the micro-credential maps to existing modules. Their time (three hours a month) is recorded in their workload plan with agreement from their heads of department. One of the lecturers is openly sceptical about assessment standards and agrees to be the critical friend.

**Practice activity: Design your champion network**

Using your stakeholder map:

1. Choose five roles for your network, including at least one sponsor and one critical friend.
2. For each role, name the type of person (a role or group is enough, not a named individual).
3. Write a short role description for one champion with the five parts: purpose, activities, time, support and recognition.
4. Plan the first three monthly calls: what will you do in each?
5. Name one risk to your network (overload, silence or echo chamber) and one step to reduce it.

This forms part B of your adoption plan.

## Communication

Champions need something to say, and every stakeholder group needs to hear the right message at the right time. This module shows how to build a communication plan that matches your stakeholder map, your champions and the stage of the programme.

### From messages to a plan

A communication plan answers six questions: Who needs to hear? What do they need to know or do? What is the message? Through which channel? Who delivers it? When?

Begin with audiences, taken from your stakeholder map. For each key group, state the change you want: a decision, an action, a different belief. "Plant managers release team leaders for two half-days" is a clear goal. "Raise awareness" is not.

Then write a core message of two or three sentences that every champion can repeat. A useful core message covers the purpose of the credential, who it is for and what happens next. Example (illustrative): "The team leader badge recognises skills our team leaders already use. It gives staff a clear way to show them and gives managers a shared standard. The pilot starts in March, and your champion can show you what it involves."

Tailor the core message for each group by changing the "what is in it for you" part, never the facts. Managers want to hear about workload and results. Teaching staff want to hear about academic quality and effort. Employee representatives want to hear about fairness and data. Earners want to hear what the badge lets them show and do.

### Choosing channels and timing

Match the channel to the purpose.

| Purpose | Suitable channels |
|---|---|
| Inform many people quickly | Intranet post, email, newsletter |
| Explain and answer questions | Team briefing, live session, open drop-in |
| Gain a decision | Short briefing paper plus meeting with the decision maker |
| Build trust and handle concern | One-to-one conversation, small group discussion |
| Show evidence | Short case, earner story, simple results summary |

Combine channels. A message that matters should reach a person in at least two ways, such as an email followed by a champion's conversation. Avoid long documents as the only channel.

Plan timing around the programme's rhythm. Typical moments are before launch, at launch, after the first cohort finishes, at the decision to continue and when the first results are available. For each moment decide who needs to be informed and who needs to be asked.

### Listening is part of communication

A plan that only sends messages will miss resistance until it is too late. Build in listening: a question box in each briefing, a standing agenda point in the champion call, a short feedback pulse after each milestone, and a place where concerns are logged and answered. Publish answers to common questions in a short FAQ. When you cannot answer a question, say so and give a date by which you will.

Be honest about what is not decided. Staff who sense that you are hiding information will fill the gaps with worse stories. State clearly what the credential will and will not be used for, especially in relation to pay, promotion and data.

## Objections

Objections are a normal part of adoption. They often contain useful information about real risks. This module teaches you to recognise the concern behind an objection, respond with respect and evidence, and decide when to change the programme.

### The concern behind the objection

Most objections fall into a small number of types. Name the type first, because the response differs.

| Type | Example | Underlying concern |
|---|---|---|
| Workload | "We cannot release people for this." | Competing priorities, lost capacity |
| Value | "Nobody will care about this badge." | Doubt about recognition |
| Quality | "Is this assessed to a proper standard?" | Academic or professional integrity |
| Fairness | "Will this be used against staff?" | Risk of unfair treatment or ranking |
| Data | "Who sees the data?" | Privacy and monitoring |
| Ownership | "We already have our own scheme." | Loss of control or duplication |
| Fatigue | "Another initiative." | Change overload |

When someone raises an objection, avoid defending at once. Use four steps:

1. **Listen and restate.** "If I understand, you worry that team leaders will be away during the busiest weeks."
2. **Ask.** "What would make it workable for you?"
3. **Respond with something real:** a change, evidence, a commitment or an honest "we do not know yet".
4. **Agree a next step** with a date.

The best answer is sometimes to change the plan. If three plant managers say the training weeks clash with peak production, the objection is data about your schedule.

### Typical objections by group

**Managers** often raise workload, value and fatigue. Useful responses: offer flexible timing, show how the badge supports an existing priority (for example, succession planning), share a short result from the pilot with its source and limits, and start with a small group.

**Teaching staff** often raise quality, ownership and workload. Useful responses: involve them in setting criteria, show how assessment is moderated, be clear about how the credential relates to existing courses, and credit their time. Avoid language that suggests the badge replaces their judgement.

**Employee representatives** often raise fairness and data. Useful responses: draft a data use statement together, state limits on use (for example, no automated ranking), agree who can see what, set a review date and make sure individuals can see their own records. Legal duties differ by country and organisation. <!-- VERIFY: add the relevant national or organisational rules on staff data and co-determination before publishing; do not cite specific articles from memory. -->

### What not to do

Four habits damage trust:

- **Promising what you cannot guarantee,** such as "the badge will definitely count for promotion".
- **Using pilot numbers as proof beyond their scope.** A pilot with 20 people shows what happened with those 20.
- **Treating objections as resistance to be overcome.** People who raise concerns often care about the organisation.
- **Making claims about competence.** A badge gives evidence of a defined learning or assessment event. It does not prove that someone will perform well in a job.

### Worked examples for objections

**Worked example: Manager objection in a corporate academy**

A plant manager at Norden Works says: "I cannot spare team leaders for two half-days. Production targets come first."

Ana follows the steps. She restates: "You are worried about shift cover during training." She asks: "Which weeks are the most difficult?" The manager names weeks 10 to 12. Ana offers to move the cohort for that plant to weeks 14 to 16 and to schedule sessions at shift handover to reduce lost time. She also asks the manager to name one team leader who would benefit most. They agree to meet again in two weeks to confirm the dates. The objection led to a better schedule.

**Worked example: Teaching staff objection in a university or provider**

A senior lecturer at Hartwell says: "This micro-credential will dilute our standards. Anyone can get a badge."

Dr Okafor restates: "You are concerned about assessment quality and the reputation of our awards." She asks: "What would you need to see to be confident?" The lecturer asks for assessment criteria, moderation and clear separation from degree credit. Dr Okafor shares the criteria, invites the lecturer to join the moderation panel and states that no degree credit is claimed until the faculty board decides. She also offers a short session for the department on how the micro-credential is quality assured.

3. Identify one objection that might lead you to change your plan, and say what you would change.

## From pilot to routine

A pilot proves little unless it leads somewhere. This module shows how to design a pilot with decision points, gather useful evidence, make the decision to continue, adapt or stop, and prepare the programme for routine operation.

### Design the pilot to answer questions

A pilot is a limited trial. Its value lies in what you learn. Before you start, write down:

- **Questions the pilot must answer,** for example: Can earners complete the credential in the time planned? Do managers see changes they value? Is the assessment workload sustainable? Is the data use acceptable?
- **Scope:** which groups, how many people, how long.
- **Evidence you will collect** and who will collect it. Use a small number of sources, such as completion records, short interviews, a brief survey and champion feedback.
- **Decision points:** when you will decide to continue, adapt or stop, and who decides.
- **Success criteria** agreed with the sponsor in advance. Do not set them after you see the results.

Keep numbers honest. A pilot of 25 people shows what happened with those 25. Label any figure with its source and size, and avoid projecting beyond them.

### The curve from pilot to routine

Adoption rarely rises in a straight line. Typical stages are: preparation, pilot, review, extension to a second wave, consolidation and routine operation. After the first excitement there is often a dip, when early champions tire and new groups have not yet been convinced. Plan for it: refresh the champion network, share results and fix the issues the pilot revealed.

### Decision points: continue, adapt or stop

At each decision point, review the evidence against the criteria agreed in advance and choose one of three paths:

- **Continue** as planned, with the next wave.
- **Adapt:** change content, timing, assessment, support or communication, then run a further wave.
- **Stop:** end the programme with a clear explanation, and keep what was learned.

Stopping is a legitimate result. A programme that ends honestly protects your credibility for the next one.

Record the decision, the evidence and the reasons in a short memo. Share it with stakeholders in the language of their interests, as in your communication plan.

### Making it routine

Routine operation means the programme no longer depends on the pilot team's extra effort. Check these elements before handover:

- **Ownership:** a named role is responsible, with a deputy.
- **Budget:** ongoing costs are in a regular budget, not a project budget.
- **Processes:** enrolment, assessment, issuing, revocation and record keeping are documented and tested.
- **Capacity:** assessors and trainers have workload time agreed.
- **Policies:** data use, privacy and fairness statements are in place and reviewed on a schedule.
- **Integration:** the badge is referred to in the systems and processes where it should matter, such as career frameworks, course catalogues or recruitment, if this has been agreed.
- **Quality:** a review cycle covers criteria, assessment and feedback, with a named owner.
- **Champions:** the network has a plan for renewal and a link to the owner.
- **Measures:** a few indicators are tracked, for example uptake, completion and feedback, and reviewed regularly.

Alignment with a quality approach helps. For recognition and credential quality, the ICoBC VRITE principles, including Impact, ask whether the programme makes a difference for earners and organisations. <!-- VERIFY: confirm the current wording of the VRITE pillars and the Impact definition from the ICoBC source before quoting. -->

### Worked examples for pilot to routine

**Worked example: Pilot to routine in a corporate academy**

Ana at Norden Works plans a six-month pilot for 25 team leaders in two plants. Questions: Can team leaders complete the badge within the planned time? Do plant managers see value? Is the trainer workload sustainable? Criteria agreed with the sponsor (illustrative): at least 80 per cent of participants complete, and at least two of the three plant managers say they would continue.

At Decision 1, completion is 76 per cent, and one plant manager supports continuing. Trainer workload is high. Ana recommends "adapt": reduce the number of assessed tasks, add a deputy trainer, and extend to a second wave of 40 people in two further plants. The sponsor agrees. She refreshes the champions with two new members and publishes a short summary of the pilot. At Decision 3 the HR department takes ownership with a regular budget, and a data use statement agreed with the works council is reviewed every year.

**Worked example: Pilot to routine in a university or training provider**

Dr Okafor runs a pilot of the data literacy micro-credential with one cohort of 30 learners. Questions: Does the assessment match the stated outcomes? Will departments accept it as a module option? Criteria: moderation confirms standards, and the faculty board agrees to consider it. After the pilot, moderators raise concerns about one assessment task. 4. Choose five items from the "making it routine" list and write one action for each, with an owner.

[(X)] A named role owns it, and costs are in a regular budget.
[( )] The pilot had no objections.
[( )] The sponsor has announced it publicly.
[[?]] Routine means it no longer depends on extra project effort.
****
Ownership and ongoing funding show that the programme is part of normal operations. The other options say nothing about sustainability.
****

**Question 4.** Early champions lose energy after the pilot, and uptake in new groups is slow. What should you do?

[( )] Treat it as failure and stop.
[( )] Wait for it to pass.
[(X)] Expect this dip, refresh the champion network and share the pilot results.
[( )] Increase the number of mandatory messages.
[[?]] This pattern is common between the pilot and the second wave.
****
A dip after the first excitement is common. Renewing champions and sharing evidence helps the programme to move forward. Waiting or sending more messages does not address the cause.
****

## Assessment and badge

### The assessed task: adoption plan for a real programme

You write an adoption plan for a real credential programme. This may be a programme you lead, one you are preparing or one you know well. You may anonymise it. The plan has five parts, which you have drafted during the course.

| Part | Content | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| A | Stakeholder map: at least eight groups, with interests, fears, influence and support, and the two groups needing most attention | 1 |
| B | Champion network: roles, a role description with purpose, activities, time, support and recognition, and a plan for the first three calls | 2 |
| C | Communication plan: core message, at least four audiences with goal, channel, messenger and timing, and a listening mechanism | 2 |
| D | Objection responses: at least three objections from different groups, with the concern, a response and a next step | 3 |
| E | Pilot-to-routine plan: pilot questions, criteria, decision points, a handover checklist and a plan for the dip | 4 |

**Length and format.** About 2,000 to 3,000 words plus tables and one diagram of your own (the stakeholder map or the adoption curve). Submit as a single document.

**Rules.** Use your real programme or a clearly labelled realistic case. Do not include personal data about named colleagues. Label all figures as illustrative or give their source. Do not claim that the badge proves job competence. A trained assessor marks the plan using the rubric below. Nothing in this file grades your work.

### Rubric

Each criterion is rated on four levels: Not yet (0), Developing (1), Secure (2), Strong (3).

| Criterion | Not yet (0) | Developing (1) | Secure (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Stakeholder map (Outcome 1) | Few groups, no interests or influence | Groups listed, interests generic | At least eight groups, specific interests and fears, influence and support stated, priorities named | As Secure, plus evidence or conversations used to test assumptions and a review plan |
| B. Champion network (Outcome 2) | No network or roles unclear | Roles named, role description incomplete | Mixed roles including a critical friend, full role description with time and recognition, plan for calls | As Secure, plus risks (overload, silence, echo chamber) addressed and renewal planned |
| C. Communication plan (Outcome 2) | Generic messages, no audiences | Audiences listed, message and channels vague | Core message, four or more audiences with goal, channel, messenger and timing, listening mechanism | As Secure, plus honest handling of open questions and use of at least two channels for key messages |
| D. Objection handling (Outcome 3) | Objections dismissed or not identified | Objections named, responses defensive or generic | Three or more objections from different groups, concern identified, respectful response, next step | As Secure, plus a case where the plan changes in response and avoidance of unsupported promises |
| E. Pilot to routine (Outcome 4) | No pilot design or decision points | Pilot described, criteria or decisions unclear | Questions, scope, criteria agreed in advance, decision points with continue, adapt or stop, handover checklist | As Secure, plus dip planned for, owners named and a review cycle |
| F. Coherence and honesty | Parts unconnected, unsupported claims | Parts linked loosely, some unlabelled figures | Parts connected to the same stakeholders, claims supported or labelled | As Secure, plus clear limits, risks and next steps, and plain, precise writing |

**Pass rule.** To earn the badge you need all of the following:

1. A rating of at least Secure (2) on criteria A, B, C, D and E.
2. A rating of at least Developing (1) on criterion F.
3. At least 75 per cent of the knowledge check below answered correctly (at least 8 of 10 questions in the version with ten questions).

A plan that does not reach the standard may be revised once and resubmitted, following the feedback from the assessor.

**Evidence submitted for the badge.** The adoption plan (parts A to E) with the diagram. The assessor's rubric record is kept with the badge evidence. The badge is "Credential Champions: Getting Adoption Inside Your Organisation (Ability, L3)", alignment code ICSF-ADPT-L3-A. The badge records that you completed the practice tasks and met the rubric standard for this task. It does not prove competence in any job role.

### Knowledge check

Answer all ten questions. Each question has one correct answer.

**Question 1.** Which of these is the best definition of a stakeholder in a credential programme?

[( )] Anyone who earns the credential.
[( )] Anyone who pays for the programme.
[(X)] Any person or group that can influence the programme or is affected by it.
[( )] Anyone who works in the programme team.
[[?]] Think broadly: influence and effect.
****
A stakeholder is anyone who can influence the programme or is affected by it. This includes earners, managers, teaching staff, enablers, representatives and external parties.
****

**Question 2.** A group has low influence and high support. Where does it sit on the stakeholder map, and what is the suitable approach?

[( )] Engage closely.
[( )] Inform and listen.
[(X)] Keep informed and use as voices.
[( )] Ignore.
[[?]] They are friendly but cannot decide.
****
Supportive groups with limited influence are best kept informed and invited to share their experience, for example through short stories or testimonials. They can help spread the message.
****

**Question 3.** What belongs in a champion role description?

[( )] Purpose, salary and holiday entitlement.
[(X)] Purpose, activities, time, support and recognition.
[( )] A list of tasks only.
[( )] Targets for the number of badges issued.
[[?]] The role needs to be realistic and acknowledged.
****
A role description with purpose, activities, time, support and recognition makes the role clear and sustainable. Time and recognition are agreed with the line manager.
****

**Question 4.** Why should a champion network include a critical friend?

[( )] To win over opponents with logic.
[( )] To reduce the programme lead's workload.
[(X)] To bring honest feedback and name problems early.
[( )] To satisfy an audit requirement.
[[?]] Enthusiasts alone create an echo chamber.
****
A critical friend helps avoid an echo chamber by telling you what is not working. This improves decisions and protects credibility.
****

**Question 5.** What is the main purpose of a core message?

[( )] To be complete and detailed.
[( )] To be different for every audience.
[(X)] To give a short, factual statement that all champions can repeat.
[( )] To sell the credential.
[[?]] Consistency across messengers.
****
A core message keeps champions consistent. Tailoring changes the emphasis, not the facts.
****

**Question 6.** Which element is essential in a communication plan, beyond sending messages?

[( )] A glossy brochure.
[(X)] A mechanism for listening and responding to questions and concerns.
[( )] A single channel to avoid confusion.
[( )] A promise about promotion outcomes.
[[?]] Two-way communication.
****
Listening shows resistance and confusion early, so you can adjust. A plan that only broadcasts will miss signals.
****

**Question 7.** A lecturer says the micro-credential will dilute standards. Which response best fits?

[( )] "Standards are not your concern."
[( )] "The sponsor has decided."
[(X)] Restate the concern, ask what would give confidence and offer a concrete step such as joining a moderation panel.
[( )] "Other universities do it already."
[[?]] Respond to the concern with something real.
****
Restating, asking and offering a practical step respects the concern and invites the lecturer into quality assurance. The other responses dismiss or deflect.
****

**Question 8.** Which statement should you avoid when answering an employee representative?

[( )] "We have not decided that yet. We will let you know by the end of the month."
[( )] "Let us write the data use statement together."
[(X)] "Badge data will never be used for anything, I guarantee it."
[( )] "Individuals will be able to see their own records."
[[?]] Beware absolute promises you cannot keep.
****
Absolute guarantees are risky if you cannot control the outcome. Honest statements about what is decided and a shared process build more trust.
****

**Question 9.** What should be agreed with the sponsor before a pilot begins?

[( )] The names of all earners.
[( )] The final design of the routine programme.
[(X)] The questions the pilot must answer and the success criteria.
[( )] The communication plan for the second year.
[[?]] This keeps the decision honest.
****
Agreeing questions and criteria in advance keeps the later decision fair and prevents setting targets to fit results.
****

**Question 10.** After a pilot, evidence shows the badge is valued but the assessment is too heavy for the available trainers. What is the best decision?

[( )] Continue unchanged, because the badge is valued.
[( )] Stop, because workload is unsustainable.
[(X)] Adapt the assessment or capacity, run a further wave and check again.
[( )] Declare routine operation and hope trainers cope.
[[?]] A fixable problem calls for adaptation and re-testing.
****
The problem is specific and fixable. Adapting and testing again protects trainers and the quality of the programme. Ignoring it would harm sustainability.
****
